Most creative tests don't fail because the ads were bad. They fail because nobody can agree on what the test actually said.

One week you look at cost per install over seven days. The next week someone checks ROAS over three. A video with a great hook gets killed because its CPA was high on day two. A static that "won" turns out to have spent forty dollars. Everyone has a different lens, so every conclusion is debatable, and the next round of production is built on opinions.

After years of running paid UA for consumer apps, this is the framework I use to read tests the same way every single week. It's simple on purpose. The goal isn't a perfect statistical model. The goal is a decision you'd make again if you saw the same data twice.

## 1. Decide what the test is for before you launch it

Every test should answer one question. Write it down before anything goes live:

- *Does a problem/solution angle beat a testimonial angle?*
- *Does a price-led hook beat a curiosity hook on the same body?*
- *Does an AI avatar perform as well as a real creator for this message?*

If you can't write the question in one line, you're not running a test. You're running a launch and hoping to learn something from it.

## 2. Change one thing at a time

The fastest way to learn nothing is to test five completely different ads against each other. When ad C wins, you don't know if it was the hook, the presenter, the length, the music or the offer.

I sort every creative variable into layers and test one layer per round:

1. **Angle:** the core reason someone should care.
2. **Hook:** the first one to three seconds.
3. **Format:** avatar, creator UGC, kinetic text, story film, static.
4. **Presenter or style:** who says it, and how it looks.
5. **Body and CTA:** the proof and the ask.
6. **Market and language.**

Angles first, because they move results the most. Then hooks, because they're the cheapest thing to vary on a winning body. The rest comes after you have something worth iterating.

## 3. Give the test a fair, fixed setup

A test is only comparable to last week's test if the conditions are the same. My defaults:

- **A dedicated test campaign**, separate from your scaling campaigns, so tests don't steal budget from proven winners or get starved by them.
- **Equal opportunity for every creative.** Either one ad set per creative with the same budget, or a structure where you watch spend distribution closely. If the algorithm gives one ad 80% of the budget on day one, you haven't tested the others.
- **The same audience and optimisation event** across every test in a series.
- **A minimum spend or event threshold per creative** before you judge it. Pick a number that makes sense for your CPI or CPA, and never make a call below it.
- **The same reading window every week.** Mine is a fixed number of days after launch, read on the same weekday. Consistency matters more than which window you choose.

## 4. Read metrics as a ladder, not a single number

One metric can't tell you why an ad won or lost. I read every creative top to bottom:

| Step | What it tells you | Common metric |
|---|---|---|
| **Stop** | Did the first second earn attention? | Hook rate: 3 second views ÷ impressions |
| **Hold** | Did the story keep them watching? | Hold rate: ThruPlays or 15 second views ÷ impressions |
| **Click** | Did the ad create intent? | CTR (link) |
| **Convert** | Did the right people act? | CPI, CPA, trial start rate |
| **Pay** | Was it worth it? | ROAS or payback on your target window |

The ladder is what turns a result into a brief. A creative with a great hook rate and weak conversion isn't a loser. It's a winning opening attached to the wrong body. A creative with a low hook rate and great conversion is a strong message nobody gets to see.

## 5. Define "winner" in writing

If the word winner means different things to different people, every report turns into a debate. Write your definition down and keep it fixed for at least a quarter. Mine has three parts:

1. **It cleared the spend threshold.** No calls on tiny samples.
2. **It beat the current benchmark on the primary KPI** (for most apps, cost per install or cost per paying user) within the fixed window.
3. **It didn't win by accident upstream.** If it has a terrible hook rate but a great CPI on very few installs, it goes into "watch", not "winner".

Everything else falls into one of four buckets, which is where the real value is.

## 6. Every creative gets a next step

A test report that just says "C won" wastes half the information. Every creative should leave the test with a decision:

- **Scale:** clear winner. Move it into the scaling campaign, and start variations immediately.
- **Variate:** promising. Keep what worked and change one thing. A strong hook with weak conversion keeps its hook and gets a new body and CTA. A strong body with a weak hook gets new openings.
- **Watch:** inconclusive or below threshold. Give it more data, or retest it in the next round.
- **Kill:** clearly worse on every rung of the ladder. Write down what you learned and move on.

The "variate" bucket is where most teams leave money on the table. A winner isn't the end of a test. It's the parent of the next five tests.

## 7. Keep a learning log, not just a results sheet

Spreadsheets of CPIs are forgettable. Learnings compound. After every test, I write one or two sentences per question:

> *"Price-led hooks beat curiosity hooks for this audience in three out of three tests. Default to price in the first two seconds."*

After a few months, that log is more valuable than any individual winner. It's also exactly what you need to brief creators and AI tools: a written list of what your audience responds to.

## 8. Mistakes I see every week

- **Calling tests too early.** Day one results are mostly noise and algorithm learning.
- **Moving the window.** If you change how you read, you can't compare to last month.
- **Testing too many variables at once.** You'll find winners but not reasons.
- **Ignoring creative fatigue.** Last quarter's winner isn't a benchmark if its frequency has doubled.
- **Never retesting.** Audiences shift. An angle that lost a year ago might win now.
- **Letting winners stall.** Finding a winner and not iterating on it within a week is the most expensive mistake in creative testing.

## The point of all this

Creative testing isn't about finding one magic ad. It's about building a loop where every test makes the next brief smarter. One question per test, one variable, one fixed setup, one written definition of a winner, and a next step for every creative.

Do that every week, and the learnings stack up much faster than your competitors' opinions.
