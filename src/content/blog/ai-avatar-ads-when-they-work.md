AI avatar ads are the most polarising format in performance marketing right now. Some people swear they've replaced UGC. Others think every avatar ad screams "fake" and burns trust.

Both camps are partly right. I've produced a lot of avatar ads for consumer apps, mostly with [Arcads](https://www.arcads.ai), and the honest answer is that avatars are a brilliant tool for some jobs and a bad one for others. This post is about telling the two apart.

## What an AI avatar ad actually is

Platforms like Arcads give you a library of AI actors: realistic people filmed in different settings, from a kitchen to a car to a bedroom. You write a script, pick an actor and a setting, and the platform generates a video of that person delivering your lines, with lip sync, emotion cues and different languages.

What used to need casting, shipping a product, waiting for a creator to film, and a round of revisions now takes minutes. That changes what's possible in testing.

## Where avatar ads genuinely win

### 1. Testing angles at volume

This is the killer use case. If you want to know whether "save money on calls abroad" beats "keep your real number private", you don't need two creators and two weeks. You need two scripts and an afternoon.

Because you can hold everything else constant (same actor, same setting, same length), avatar ads are one of the cleanest ways to isolate the message itself. That's exactly what a good creative test wants.

### 2. Hook testing on a fixed body

Write one body and ten different openings. Generate all ten with the same actor. Now you're testing hooks, not people. When a hook wins, you know why.

### 3. Localisation

Taking a winning script into new markets with native-sounding delivery used to be one of the slowest parts of international UA. With avatars, a winning angle can be in several languages the same week. When you're running campaigns across many countries, this alone pays for the tool.

### 4. Explainer-style ads

When the job is to explain how an app works in thirty seconds ("here's how I got a second number in two taps"), a calm presenter talking to camera with screen recordings cut in is a format avatars handle well. The viewer is paying attention to the product, not the person.

### 5. Filling the gaps between creator content

Real creator videos take time. Avatars keep the testing pipeline full while you wait, and they let you pre-test the angles you'll brief to creators next.

## Where avatar ads look cheap

### 1. When trust is the product

Categories where the viewer needs to believe a real person had a real experience (health, finance, dating, anything with personal stakes) are where synthetic presenters struggle most. Viewers read authenticity cues fast, and an avatar that feels slightly off can cost you more trust than it earns attention.

### 2. Emotional storytelling

Avatars are good at delivering lines. They're weaker at the micro-expressions, pauses and imperfections that make a personal story land. If your angle is emotional, a real creator will usually win.

### 3. Over-polished scripts

The fastest way to make an avatar ad look like an ad is to give it ad copy. "Introducing the revolutionary app that transforms your communication" sounds wrong coming from someone sitting on their bed. People don't talk like landing pages.

### 4. Everyone using the same faces

Popular actors in popular settings show up across many brands' ads. If your audience has seen the same face selling three other apps this week, you start with a credibility deficit. Rotate actors, and avoid the obvious first picks.

### 5. Long, static talking heads

Thirty seconds of one person talking, with nothing else on screen, is boring whether the person is real or not. Avatar ads without edit work tend to fatigue fast.

## My workflow for avatar ads that feel native

### Write scripts like speech, not copy

I write every script out loud. Short sentences. Contractions. A little hesitation. Emotion cues where a real person would react: a sigh before the problem, a laugh at the absurd part, a lean in for the reveal. A script should sound like a voice note to a friend.

### One hook mechanic per script

Every script in a batch gets a different hook type: a confession, a question, a hot take, a "stop doing this", a mini story, a demo. If they all open the same way, you're not testing anything.

### Product proof on screen, always

An avatar saying "it's easy" is a claim. A screen recording of it actually being easy is proof. I cut real app footage into every avatar ad, usually right after the hook, and show the key moment (the number appearing, the call connecting, the result screen) clearly.

### Captions, b-roll and pattern breaks

Burned-in captions for sound-off viewing. A zoom or cut every few seconds. B-roll that illustrates what's being said. The goal is that the avatar is one ingredient of the edit, not the whole thing.

### Match the actor to the audience and the setting to the story

If the script is about calling family abroad, the actor and setting should look like someone who would do that. A mismatch between who's speaking and what they're saying is one of the first things viewers notice.

### Check the details that give it away

Before anything ships, I review every clip for the common tells: lip sync drift, odd hand movements, a brand name pronounced with a strange accent (fix it with an on-screen logo pop at that moment), and any claim that isn't on the brand's fact sheet.

### Label and stay within platform policies

Platforms have their own rules on AI-generated and synthetic content, including when it needs to be disclosed or labelled. Check the current policies for every platform you run on, and keep your claims honest: an AI presenter shouldn't pretend to have personal experiences they couldn't have had.

## How avatars and creators work together

The best results I've seen don't come from choosing between avatars and creators. They come from a loop:

1. **Avatars test angles and hooks** cheaply and quickly.
2. **The winning angle becomes the brief** for real creators.
3. **Creators make the version only a human can make**, which often has a much higher ceiling.
4. **Avatars iterate on the creator winner**: new hooks, new markets, new languages.

I wrote more about the human side of this loop in [AI Made Ads Cheap. That's Exactly Why UGC Matters More Than Ever](/blog/why-ugc-matters-more-in-the-age-of-ai).

## The short version

Use AI avatars for **volume, speed, clean tests, explainers and localisation**. Be careful with them for **trust-heavy categories, emotional stories and anything that sounds like ad copy**. And never let an avatar carry the whole edit on its own: real product proof, good captions and a script written like speech are what make it feel native.
